Transit delivery is a policy variable

A new line's timeline isn't physics — most of it is review, procurement, and management overhang that peer countries don't carry. Compress that overhang and you open transit-accessible housing capacity years sooner, cheaper, and at greater scale. This model runs on its own clock and hands its results to the rental model.

Where the numbers come from — empirical anchors

The premise — that delivery time and cost are policy-driven, not fixed — rests on the cross-country construction record.

  1. Transit Costs Project — 2026 data (NYU Marron Institute): weighted average ~$252M/km worldwide; the US is "expensive and slow," China and India "cheap and fast" — cost and speed are correlated, distinct axes.
  2. Second Avenue Subway case study: ~$2.5B/mile, 8–12× comparable lines in Italy, Istanbul, Sweden, Paris, Berlin, and Spain — the premium is regulatory, procurement, and management, not land.
  3. Eno Transit Construction Cost Database: the US premium concentrates in tunneled projects; at-grade US lines are roughly in line with European costs.
  4. Transit Costs Project — Final Report: the premium decomposes into multiplicative factors — physical structures (stations & tunneling), labor, and procurement & soft costs — that compound; NYC station methods alone tripled station costs through overbuilding.
  5. Vital City: SAS Phase 1 station contracts were 77% of construction cost; station boxes ran 60% longer than platforms vs 3–20% abroad. Soft costs were 21% for SAS vs 7–8% in low-cost cases.
  6. Interborough Express: 14 mi, 19 stations, ~$5.5B, mostly at-grade on repurposed freight tracks, early-2030s — the built-in example.
  7. Vital City (2026) and Construction Dive: SAS Phase 2 at ~$4.3B/mile; US rail runs ~50% costlier than Europe/Canada on average, ~250% including NYC.

Calibration spans that record: cost/mile ~$1B (frontier, at-grade) to ~$5–6B (status quo, fully tunneled); delivery ~6 to ~15 years, with a physical-build floor that speed cannot cross. Illustrative and directional — a reasoning aid, not a project estimate.

How this connects. Transit runs on a decade-scale, spatial clock the rental model deliberately doesn't carry. Rather than fuse the two, this tool computes transit's housing consequences and hands them across a clean interface: a bump to the rental model's permit floor, a bump to its suburban build, a utilization multiplier on transit-zone rezoning, and the year they arrive. Run a line here, carry the four numbers over there.
