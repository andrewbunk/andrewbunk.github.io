NYC rental supply simulator

The trajectory starts today (2026) and runs to 2048. Set a durable permit rate and rezoning uplift, then watch when vacancy troughs, when real rents begin easing, and when relief reaches the low-rent tier. Vacancy is anchored at the latest official reading — 1.4% (2023 HVS) — against ~2.3M rental units. The record 2024–25 completion surge is already behind us; its tail still flows into 2026–29 completions.

Policies

Enacted and proposed reforms as a modular layer. Toggling one applies its effect on top of your slider baseline and moves the affected sliders on the Supply and Quality tabs, so you can see the mechanism. Effects stack; the sliders show the resolved result.

Each policy acts only through channels this model tracks (permits, rezoning, approval speed, demand, margin, warehouse, migration). A policy whose real effect falls outside those channels is listed with a near-zero effect and a note — shown, not faked.

Model internals

The calibration constants every scenario rests on. Editing one re-runs all three tabs live. These are modeling choices, not policy — the honest uncertainty lives here, and the preset buttons never touch them.

Edits persist across preset changes until you reset. Pushing values to extremes can break the model's plausibility — being able to see that is the point.

What the model tends to teach — a few lessons

These fall out of the model's structure across many runs. They're the shape of what it argues, not forecasts — and each carries the model's own blind spots.

  1. The trough is a crossover, not a build date. Vacancy keeps falling for years after you start building; it only turns when completions net of losses overtake household growth. "Construction caught up" and "rents eased" are years apart.
  2. Permits are the floor; completions are a lagging echo. Today's high completions are the 2022 permit surge arriving late. The durable permit rate — not the completion headline — sets the mid-2030s.
  3. Rezoning raises the ceiling, not the floor. It creates capacity; only utilization turns capacity into units. Upzoning is necessary but not sufficient.
  4. A freeze without building shifts the shortage's cost onto the unregulated tier. Holding supply fixed, a freeze protects stabilized tenants but thins margin — raising market rents, growing the warehouse, and eroding condition. Out-migration pressure lands on the exposed ~60%, so the freeze concentrates displacement on those it doesn't cover. Crucially, this is a supply effect: pair the same freeze with aggressive upzoning and the displacement largely disappears. The lesson is "freezing as a substitute for building," not "freezing" alone.
  5. Relief through out-migration is displacement, and the suburbs gate it. Rents can plateau because people are priced out to LI/NJ — but only while the ring has room. When it fills, the valve closes and people stay and absorb the increase. A falling rent line can hide a rising human cost.
  6. Low-rent relief lags citywide relief by many years. New market-rate supply reaches the bottom tier only through slow filtering, so the tier with the worst scarcity is the last to loosen.
  7. Price and quality can move in opposite directions. Cheaper is not automatically better: a freeze can lower rents on paper while condition decays and units vanish into the warehouse. Any single "rent" metric hides this.

Two standing blind spots behind every lesson: the model scores displacement but not the stabilized tenants a freeze protects from being displaced — so it is structurally unkind to the freeze — and it scores rent timing, not the value to a sitting tenant of a rent that never rose. Read it as a map of trade-offs, not a verdict on any policy.

Where the defaults come from — empirical anchors

The structure here is standard housing economics; each default is anchored to the published studies below. Every one is a range, not a point — which is why they're editable.

Rent ↔ vacancy (b, v*) & adjustment speed

  1. Rosen & Smith (1983), "The Price Adjustment Process for Rental Housing and the Natural Vacancy Rate," American Economic Review 73:779–786.
  2. Hendershott, Estimation of the Rental Adjustment Process.
  3. Gabriel & Nothaft, Rental Housing Markets, the Incidence and Duration of Vacancy, and the Natural Vacancy Rate.
  4. The rent–vacancy relationship in the rental housing market (ScienceDirect).
  5. Kumar (2024), A Search and Matching Model of Housing and Rental Market Interactions.
  6. Rydell (1982), Price Elasticities of Housing Supply (RAND/HUD).
  7. Rental Vacancy Rates by City and State — the 5–8% balanced band (Census HVS).

Induced demand / doubling-up (ε)

  1. Haurin, Hendershott & Kim (1993), "The Impact of Real Rents and Wages on Household Formation," Review of Economics and Statistics.
  2. JCHS (2025), Affordability Pressures and Fewer Young Adults Strain Household Formation.
  3. Terner Center, Who Is Actually Forming New Households?
  4. SF Fed (2016), Household Formation among Young Adults.
  5. Federal Reserve (2022), The Remarkable Recent Rebound in Household Formation.
  6. BLS Monthly Labor Review (2013), Housing/labor-market crises and doubling up.

Out-migration (μ) & income anchoring

  1. OECD (2021), The Laws of Attraction: inter-regional migration, housing costs and policies — 10% price growth → ~2% fewer inflows.
  2. Howard, How Regional Inequality and Migration Drive Housing Prices and Rents (JEP) — population-rent elasticity ≈ ⅓ of population-income elasticity.
  3. Saiz (2003), Immigration and Housing Rents in American Cities (IZA DP 2189).
  4. Federal Reserve (2020), Housing Supply and Affordability: Evidence from Rents (Davis & Ortalo-Magné rent share; Diamond migration elasticity).

Permit → completion lag (NYC)

  1. NYC Rent Guidelines Board, 2025 Housing Supply Report — most permitted projects complete within four years.
  2. NYC Dept. of City Planning, Development Pipeline documentation.
  3. NYC Council / HPD (Mar 2024), testimony on pipeline timelines — 1 to 5+ years.

Operating margin, costs & condition

  1. NYC Rent Guidelines Board — Income & Expense Studies (operating costs as a share of rent).
  2. From Workplace to Residence: Spillover Effects of Minimum Wage Policies on Local Housing Markets — the wage-cost → rent channel.
  3. HPD code-violation records and NYC 311 heat/hot-water complaints — condition proxy.

These validate the model's shape and the order of magnitude of each default — not a fitted NYC calibration. A rigorous version would estimate b, ε, and μ on NYC panel data (HVS + ACS) with an instrument for supply (e.g. lagged zoning changes). Until then, treat outputs as directional.

How to read it. Today is 2026. The 2026–29 completions still carry the tail of the 2022 permit surge, so they start elevated and fall toward your permit floor before rezoning fills back in. The condition track and the warehouse (on the second tab) both run on landlord margin: a rent freeze with rising costs thins that margin, bending condition down and pushing vacant units into the warehouse, while preservation spending and a higher IAI cap push the other way. Condition is a proxy (think code violations and heat complaints), not a hard measure, and the warehouse size is genuinely disputed — sweep it. Out-migration eases rents by pricing people out, not by satisfying them — so the "households priced out" figure is the cost behind that relief, and it's gated by how much room Long Island and New Jersey have (set on the Model internals tab). All figures are illustrative, calibrated to public 2023–26 data — a reasoning aid, not a forecast.
